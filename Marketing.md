# Marketing

> "It's a car signaler! No, it's a smart light controller! No, it's a gaming console! No, it's a musical instrument! Neh, it's just RGB Strip."
> — with apologies to Superman

## The anti-salesman stance

The Strip is just a lightstrip — one of millions out there. It doesn't need to be sold; it just needs to exist as a branded option. In a sea of anonymous components, "The Strip" with a name, logo, and identity becomes the default choice.

There's no advertising, no sales pitch, no psychological pricing tricks. The back of the box does all the work — turn it over, and you see eight panels, game screenshots, the entire platform explained. You either grin and buy it, or put it back. Both outcomes are fine.

Social media is the only propagation channel. A video from @strip.rgb — logo, identity, no commentary — showing a controller, a panel snapping on, and Cars 2D playing on a strip of LEDs. It's a mic drop without words. The branding tells you immediately: this is an official product, not some hobbyist prototype.

The hardware is honest — an ESP32, batteries, neopixels. Everyone knows what these cost. Price it fairly and people feel smart buying it. Then the accessories become the natural next step: gamers buy the full arcade set, musicians buy the EE panel, car enthusiasts buy two 4M strips. Each accessory is a cheap, optional add-on — a bare PCB with buttons, small and collectible like cartridges.

If something is expensive for what it's worth, people won't buy it. If it's cheap enough, they'll buy two. That's not marketing — it's just building a product people actually want. No "limited editions," no manufactured scarcity — just a catalog with honest prices, and a customer who feels like they discovered something themselves.

**No decimal pricing tricks** — Prices are round numbers — $10, $50, $200. No $9.99, no $199.90, no decimal tricks.

**No hidden trap tricks** — The product is complete at every level. A strip with an IR remote or the mobile app is a fully working system. Controllers, panels, and accessories are upgrades, never requirements. Nobody is ever forced to buy something they don't need.

## Why so many SKUs?

The abundance of strictly defined SKUs isn't a logistical problem — it's the platform's greatest strength, because a SKU is not a box on a shelf; it's a frozen, verified recipe that only materialises when someone actually wants it. Modern PCB services like JLCPCB, PCBWay, and 3D printing make on-demand manufacturing trivial, with zero inventory risk — like a gourmet chef who can cook any of 300 courses on demand without preparing anything in advance.

Dozens of panels, extensions, and displays are designed, documented, and available from day one. The extensive SKU list proves the platform's versatility, invites tinkering, and assures users that whatever crazy idea they have, there's probably already a spec for it. Order the official branded version for convenience, or build from the open documentation. Either way, the platform grows stronger.
