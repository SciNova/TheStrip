# 26 Nov 2026 – Claude Sonnet 5 – Presence planning, a from-scratch synth scale system, the menu's exact-fill layout, a Semaphore built three times over, and Race's travel/lap model corrected

We opened with a presence/marketing conversation, not code: the `@strip.rgb` TikTok account's `Videos` table in `Development.md` sat at only two entries, both from January, which led to talking through why that matters now — competing games built on generic LED strips are becoming common, and the brand's actual differentiator (a platform, not a one-off game) only reads as real if it's visible. We settled on a content strategy of small, non-spoiler mechanism clips (the rotating-band circle from `Test.js`, the fisheye shadow trick shared between `Dino`/`Golf`) rather than leading with finished games, reusing the project's own habit of recording working sessions as raw footage. We also worked out a caption voice for these videos: flat, third-person, factual by default (state a non-obvious rule or name a result), with a looser first-person aside we reserved specifically for the rare moment a factual line would just restate what the footage already shows, or as a direct reaction to a fact just stated. We drafted a full two-round caption script for a Race video as a concrete test of the idea:

Round 1 — "let's go baby!" (the loose-voice opener, since the footage already shows the race starting); "the car advances by repeatedly pressing the button" (factual, the mechanic); "the yellow bars in the back are the progress of the cars" (factual, naming the UI); "host won" (flat result).

Round 2 — "oh wait, I'm the guest, I'll try harder this time" (loose-voice, a meta realization doubling as the round's opener, replacing the need for a second "let's go"); the fact that holding the button does nothing, it has to be pressed rapidly (agreed as content, exact wording never finalized); one loose-voice beat reacting to that fact, still undecided between two drafted options (an arcade-buttons aside, or a line about going all-in/no more holding back) — we deliberately kept only one of the two, not both, to keep round 2's rhythm matching round 1's four-beat shape rather than stacking three personality lines against only one fact; "guest won" (flat result, mirroring round 1, not re-confirmed in so many words but implied by the parallel structure).

We also connected the earlier resolution-flexibility work (lower resolutions framing better in the 3D view) to this same presence push — the simulator's camera limitations turned out to matter for video quality, not just development.

### Slots

We set out to make a miss silent (dropping `BOOING`, keeping `CHEERING` on a win) and give each fruit its own note on stop. We landed on a shared `Scale` constant added to `core/audio-common.js`, not Slots-specific — five real scale families (Major, Minor, the double-harmonic/Hijaz shape nicknamed "Arabic," a pentatonic "Chinese," and a stacked-major-thirds "Scary" with no true resolution), each stored as a plain two-octave array of `Note`s rooted at `C4`, with no transposition baked in, so any app can pick its own root and however many notes it needs. Slots takes the first 5 entries of whichever scale is selected (switchable via `LEFT`/`RIGHT`, mirroring Simon's existing note-set mechanism) and maps them onto the 5 actually-used fruits, ordered by ascending `Fruit` enum id; we gave the 4 unused fruits the remaining positions, same ordering rule, for future reuse. Each wheel now plays a standalone `play2('SAWTOOTH', ...)` tone the instant it stops, not reusing any existing patch, with a real sustain level so `duration` actually governs how long each tone rings.

### Menu layout

The system menu's game-picker layout used to divide the strip into `games.length + 1` equal-width slots, one of them a gap. We reworked it so every pair of adjacent entries gets exactly a 1px gap, with a single larger gap — sized to absorb all rounding slack — between the apps and games groups, and derived it so apps start flush at pixel 0 and games end flush at the last pixel; we checked by hand that it sums exactly to the strip length at every live resolution (120/60/40/30), with no leftover or shortfall at any of them. We also added a short sine beep on `LEFT`/`RIGHT` menu navigation, and wired `UP`/`DOWN` to behave identically to `LEFT`/`RIGHT`/`A`/`B` for browsing.

### `isGameButtonPress` → `isGameCommand`

We flagged it as a hack: it matched "any real button press" via a numeric range check plus a bit-parity trick on the `Command` encoding, rather than naming what it actually meant. We replaced it with a plain literal list of the real command values, renamed it `isGameCommand`, and updated all five JS callers (`FlappyBird`, `Race`, `Slots`, `Dices`, `Dino`). We deliberately left the C++ `.h` equivalent alone — it operates on an entirely different `Action` bit layout, a separate decision for another day.

### 3D view

We found the 3D view's LED pitch was a flat, invented `5mm` constant regardless of resolution, and corrected it into a real `density` constructor parameter (LEDs/meter, default 144) with pitch derived as `1000/density`; at the default this yields the correct ~0.83m physical length for a 120-pixel strip, matching the real product. We corrected the LED footprint from 4×4mm to 5×5mm, the backing from 5mm to 6mm wide. We replaced the flat gray/colored-grid floor we'd started with, on request, with a tiled, code-drawn brand floor: the real "STRIP" wordmark, in the real font and the real background-blue, drawn via `FontFace` + `Canvas` with no pre-rendered image asset, alternating upright and upside-down with a half-row offset — a genuine brick/masonry pattern baked into one repeating compound tile rather than a shader. The tile rotates in-plane to read along the strip's own length, anchors flush against the strip's true edge, and uses a muted, same-hue-darker text over the brand blue. We removed the camera's old `1000mm` hard zoom-out cap entirely, now unbounded and limited only by the renderer's far clipping plane.

### Race bug fixes

We found and fixed three bugs, unrelated to any of the day's larger rework. `playerA`'s color diverged between the constructor (`RED`) and the round-reset block (`GREEN`), a leftover mismatch from an earlier session — we resolved it by making both rounds consistently red/green. We found the boost input gated to one specific button per pad (`Command.A`/`Command.C`, both of which happen to alias their own `_PRESS` constants, so it did technically work, just far too narrowly) — we opened it up to any real game command via `isGameCommand`. We found the trail's brightness gradient always put the brightest pixel at array index 0, but which end of that array actually renders at the car's current position depends on `Alignment` (`FORWARD` vs `BACKWARD`), so the bright end only tracked the car correctly in one of the two travel directions; we fixed it by deriving the head index from the active alignment instead of hardcoding it.

### Semaphore

We added a pre-race countdown — a `Semaphore` class with states `OFF → FADE_IN → RED → RED2 → YELLOW → GREEN → FADE_OUT`, cumulative lighting (each light stays lit as later ones join, not a single bulb cycling), a real crossfade in and out of whatever's already on the strip, and placement that mirrors the player-staging side: a single `inverted` flag shared between where players stage for a round and which end of the strip the semaphore occupies and which way it mirrors its own light order, anchored on green's own outermost pixel and walking inward from there with real single-pixel gaps, via a `lightSize` getter (`Math.floor((this.size - 3) / 4)`) that deliberately reads the semaphore's own general `size` rather than hardcoding resolution math inside the getter.

Getting there took two real wrong turns, and we reverted both in full rather than patching over them. We first built it as a generic, table-driven state machine — module-level constant objects for duration-per-state, color-per-state, sequence order, beep duration — and we rejected that outright as needless indirection for something that should just do the correct thing at the correct time, explicitly, where it happens. We built the second attempt correctly, as an explicit `if`/`else if` chain reading a per-state elapsed timer, but it still had a design error underneath: we reset `stateTime` at every transition, when the actual intent was a single fixed `timestamp` set once at the very start, with every transition checked against a *cumulative* elapsed time from that one instant. The final shape has no shared tables and one `render()` method covering both solid and fade-blend output in a single pass with no second scratch buffer — an earlier version computed a full "what would this look like solid" copy via `pixels.slice()` purely to blend against, and we reverted that too as unnecessary once it became clear a solid render visits no more pixels than a blended one does, so each visited pixel's own color can just be computed inline and blended directly against the live buffer. The standing lesson, worth carrying forward: a method taking an extra parameter, or a derived property quietly reading from the wrong source, is exactly the kind of structural decision that needs surfacing before it's written, not just behavior that happens to look right once tested.

### Player and `lap`

We found `Player.tick(dt)` took an explicit `dt` parameter, inconsistent with every other class in the codebase, which reads `system.clock.delta` directly, and fixed it, pre-existing code though it was. We also found `_resetRound()`, a private helper we'd invented for rebuilding a round's players and semaphore, duplicated an existing hook: `Game.playLevel()` already calls an optional `buildStage()` override and handles the rest, so we renamed it and rewired both call sites to use the real entry point instead of our own invented one.

The deeper fix: since players now stage off-center rather than starting exactly at the strip's middle, `travel` — and therefore the win condition, previously `travel >= targetTravel` — needed a real starting line, not just a spawn point. We gave `Player` a `lap` attribute: `travel` doesn't accumulate at all until the player's position first crosses the strip's true center, at which point `lap` becomes `1` and travel starts counting from zero; from there, `lap` advances by exactly one each time travel crosses the next `2×resolution` boundary, landing on `11` at exactly the distance the original 10-lap win condition already expected — we changed the number, not the actual win distance. We switched both call sites (`Race.loop()`'s win trigger, `_drawResultBars()`) from the old `travel`-based check to `lap > Race.TOTAL_LAPS`; we also simplified `_drawResultBars()` at the same time, from a three-branch win/tie structure down to two independent one-line `if`s, since a true simultaneous tie needs no special case of its own — two independent checks already produce it for free. We wired the `CHECKPOINT` sound to play directly at the two places `lap` is actually modified (the center-crossing and each lap-boundary advance), with no before/after comparison pattern anywhere, and removed the one pre-existing case of it (`TICK` retains its own, for a much finer-grained, continuously-sampled boundary where some comparison is unavoidable).

### Widget

We generalized `Semaphore`'s own lifecycle into a reusable base class, `Widget`, in `core/graphics.js`, since a pre-race countdown and an on-screen result display turned out to be the same underlying shape: something with a `location`, a `size`, an `inverted` orientation, and a visibility lifecycle (`OFF → FADE_IN → ON → FADE_OUT`) driven by `show()`/`hide()`/`fadeIn()`/`fadeOut()`. The base owns every piece of that lifecycle — `state`, `alpha`, `timestamp`, and `startTime` (stamped once, exactly when `state` reaches `ON`, from either `show()` or the `FADE_IN → ON` transition, polymorphically, so a derived class like `Semaphore` can override `start()` itself to fire a beep at that exact moment with no other wiring needed) — while a derived widget never touches any of that machinery directly; it only calls the entry points it wants and reads `state`/`alpha` back.

Rendering split into two differently-named methods for a reason specific to inheritance, not a style choice: `Widget.render()` owns the actual pixel sweep (from `location`, `size` pixels, direction set by `inverted`), and calls `renderPixel(i)` — a pure function of the local index `i`, implemented by each derived widget — to get each pixel's color. We first assumed this could mirror the audio side's `render()` convention, then realized the audio case is composition (a container calling `render()` on whatever object it holds), while this is inheritance (one method name, one slot, shared up and down one chain) — JavaScript can't let a base's zero-argument `render()` and a derived class's one-argument version coexist under the same name, so the per-pixel version needed its own name, `renderPixel`.

Blending got its own small pieces rather than being baked into `render()`'s pixel loop: `Game.blend(index, value, alpha)`, next to `clear()`, holds the actual channel-lerp math; `Widget.blend`, a plain boolean defaulting to `false`, is a per-instance opt-in for whether a widget's pixels blend against whatever's already there or just overwrite it solid. Any future show/hide effect — a slide, a zoom, a shatter — is just another pair of methods on `Widget` driving its own bit of state the same way `fadeIn`/`fadeOut` drive `alpha`; a derived widget calls whichever one it needs and never needs to know how it works. `ProgressBar` only ever calls `show()` once and is never faded at all; `Semaphore` is driven by `fadeIn()`/`fadeOut()`; `Result` later proved a widget can mix entry points freely, fading in and (hypothetically) leaving some other way, since neither knows about the other.

One real, non-obvious bug came out of generalizing the sweep direction: we initially carried over `step = inverted ? 1 : -1` from `Semaphore`'s own original convention, where `false` happened to sweep backward — but a base class's default (`inverted = false`) should mean the plain, forward direction, not the reverse of it. We corrected the base to `step = inverted ? -1 : 1` and, because every existing caller had been tuned against the old convention, had to flip the `inverted` flag at each one to preserve their actual on-strip behavior: `Semaphore` now passes `!this.inverted` instead of `this.inverted`, and `ProgressBar`'s two instances swapped their flags. The standing lesson: a base class's default behavior should read as the obviously normal case on its own terms, not whatever happened to fall out of the first thing it was extracted from.

### ProgressBar

We added `ProgressBar` as `Widget`'s second real use, replacing `Race`'s inline `_drawProgressBar` fill logic: a `value` field, `0` to `1`, set directly by `Race` each frame with no setter method, and a `renderPixel(i)` that just compares `i` against `value * size`. It never fades — `Race` simply never calls anything but `render()` on it — which on its own proved the lifecycle methods are opt-in, not mandatory scaffolding every widget has to engage with. The host and guest bars sit at fixed ends of the strip (`location = 0` and `location = resolution - 1`), never swapping with `Race`'s own round-to-round `inverted` flag the way the semaphore and player staging do, since the host is always the host regardless of which side it's physically racing from that round.

### Result

We added a third widget, `Result`, to replace `_drawResultBars()`: full-strip size, a centered third showing each side's color (dark by default, flashing bright every `0.15s` for the winning side only, steady dark for a non-winner), with everything else black. Two real bugs surfaced building it, both worth keeping as settled knowledge:

The first: blending a widget against `pixels` only works if what's underneath is re-rendered fresh every frame before the widget draws on top of it. We'd skipped re-rendering the frozen race scene while `Result` was up, on the reasoning that nothing needed to change there — but `Game.blend()` reads whatever is currently in `pixels`, which by then was last frame's *already-blended* output, not the true background; blending toward the same target with a shrinking `alpha` against an already-near-target pixel barely moves it, so the widget looked like it snapped off instead of fading. The fix wasn't a snapshot buffer — it was restoring the obvious rule: render order determines what's behind what, so the background still gets rendered every single frame, same as always, with the widget simply drawn last, on top.

The second: once we moved to building the *next* round's stage immediately (so it's already sitting there, ready, by the time `Result` finishes fading out, rather than revealing the just-finished race), `System`'s own `loadGame()` turned out to already call `playLevel()` once on every freshly constructed game — meaning `Race`'s own constructor calling it a second time was always redundant, harmless only because the old `buildStage()` happened to both build and start a stage in one step. Once we split "build" from "start" (so the next stage can be built early without starting its countdown), that redundancy broke: the second, external `playLevel()` call rebuilt a fresh, never-started semaphore that silently replaced the one the constructor had started, and the race never left `COUNTDOWN`. The fix: `buildStage()` only ever builds, full stop; `Race` overrides `playLevel()` itself to add the "start the countdown" step on top of the base behavior, so the one real entry point used for every fresh load — construct, then `playLevel()` — always does both, exactly once.

Since the next stage's players are now built (and their `lap`/`travel` reset to zero) before `Result` ever finishes displaying, `Result` can't read the winner off `Race.playerA`/`playerB` by the time it renders — those already point at the new round's fresh players. We capture the finishing `travel` values into two plain numbers, `hostTravel`/`guestTravel`, at the exact moment the round ends, and hand those to `Result` directly, rather than a full `Player` reference or anything resembling the game's own live fields.

### Player travel and lap, revisited

We found a second real bug in the `travel`/`lap` model from earlier in the session: `travel` only started accumulating once `lap` left `0`, which meant the dedicated "did we cross center" check and the general per-lap `travel` threshold were two separate mechanisms for what's really the same event, and also meant the `TICK` sound — which reads `travel`'s own boundary crossings — never fired at all during a player's run from its staggered starting position to the strip's actual center. We removed the dedicated position-crossing check entirely and pre-loaded each player's `travel` to a negative value in `buildStage()` — the signed distance from its starting position to center — then let `travel` accumulate unconditionally, every tick, from the start. `travel` now crosses zero at exactly the same moment the old explicit check used to fire, so the single `travel >= lap * resolution * 2` threshold now drives every lap advance uniformly, including the first, and `TICK` — needing no change of its own — started firing correctly for free.
