# 17 May 2026 (c) – DeepSeek 3 – Race rebuilt on NewDisplayObject, terminology lock, edge modes, progress bars, checkpoint audio

### Terminology Lock: Position, Location, Travel

We began by tackling the quiet naming confusion that had been growing across the codebase. Three words were hammered into permanent definitions. **Position** is now strictly a point on the LED strip – an index between 0 and 119 – and it is always passed into `render()` as a parameter, never stored inside an object. **Location** is a game‑world coordinate, completely independent of the display. In a 1:1 world like Race it happens to equal the position, but in a scrolling world like Dino it can run to tens of thousands of pixels without ever touching the strip directly. **Travel** is the absolute, monotonic odometer – the true pixel distance a player has moved, accumulated frame by frame from exact changes in location. It is the single source of truth for scoring and progress. With those three terms locked, a great deal of potential confusion about coordinates simply evaporated from the platform.

### NewDisplayObject and EdgeMode

With the vocabulary settled we introduced the new display base class, `NewDisplayObject`, into `App.hx`. It was placed carefully beside the original `DisplayObject` – no game that uses the old class is touched or broken. The constructor takes only `(app, size, ?alignment = CENTER)`. There is no position field. The `render(position:Float, ?edge:EdgeMode = CLIP)` method blits the object’s local pixel buffer at the given position, using whatever alignment the object currently holds, and applying the global `correctLuminance` to every non‑black pixel. The alignment field is public and mutable, so a derived object can flip it during `update()` when its orientation changes – no subclass overrides required, the base class is final and trusted.

To handle what happens when drawn pixels cross the ends of the strip we created the `EdgeMode` enum with three values. `CLIP` is the default and simply drops pixels that fall outside the 0–119 window – exactly the right behaviour for most games. `MIRROR` bounces pixels back at the edges, matching the physics of Race where the players and their trails reflect off the ends. `WRAP` makes the strip toroidal, so pixels that leave one side reappear on the other, already earmarked for Pacman. The mode is chosen per `render()` call by the game loop, not by the object. That separation keeps the base class clean and the caller fully in control of the world model.

### Player Rewritten

Player was completely rebuilt to extend `NewDisplayObject`. It now carries `location`, `speed`, `reverse`, `travel`, and `color`. The `update()` method handles the full lifecycle of the racer. It applies friction and boost physics, moving the player along the strip. When the player hits a boundary the location is reflected and the direction toggled – the same bounce behaviour, now perfectly aligned with the `MIRROR` edge mode that will be used during rendering. Travel is updated by taking the exact absolute difference between the old and new location; no approximation, no drift. The method then sets `this.alignment` to `FORWARD` or `BACKWARD` based on the `reverse` flag, so the trail always extends opposite to the direction of motion. Finally it fills the object’s own pixel buffer with a 20‑pixel gradient. The head pixel gets full brightness, the tail fades down to 127 intensity, and the whole thing is multiplied by the player’s own colour. A long‑standing bug was fixed during this rewrite: the gradient had been accidentally inverted, making the head dim and the tail bright. Another bug disappeared too – when the player stopped moving the trail shrunk to zero length, causing a division by zero in the intensity calculation. Now a single‑pixel trail is always visible at full brightness.

The old external `drawTrail()` function in the Race class is gone entirely. The game loop no longer knows anything about trail rendering. It simply calls `player.update()` and then `player.render(player.location, MIRROR)`.

### Progress Bars and the Finish Line

Two‑player scoring became a set of dim yellow background bars. Each player owns one half of the strip: Player A on the left, Player B on the right. The bar length for each is `score × half`, where `score = travel / (TOTAL_LAPS × 2 × resolution)`. As a player moves, their bar grows from their outer edge toward the centre. The exact centre two pixels are always lit in the same yellow, giving both players a permanent visual target. When a bar reaches the middle, that player has won.

The score itself runs from 0.0 to 1.0. A full lap is 240 pixels of travel – from centre to one end, across to the other end, and back to centre – so the total target distance for a race is `TOTAL_LAPS × 2 × resolution`. All of this fell naturally out of the travel odometer and required no extra tracking.

### Race State Machine

A simple two‑state machine governs the game flow. `PLAYING` is the active state: players update and render, progress bars draw, and the loop runs freely. The moment one player’s travel reaches the target, the state switches to `RESULT`. The crowd cheer (`LEVEL_COMPLETE`) fires, the screen clears, and the winner’s half of the strip is filled with their luminance‑corrected colour. If both players somehow cross the line in the same frame with equal travel – a perfect tie – both halves light up in their respective colours. No winner field is stored; the determination is computed directly from the players’ travel values on the spot. After a four‑second defer, new players are created and the state returns to `PLAYING`.

### Engine Audio and Checkpoints

The race gained an audible rhythm. `TICK` fires every 10 pixels of travel for each player using a simple integer‑division boundary check on the previous and current travel. No extra variables, no timers, just the existing travel accumulator. The same pattern drives `CHECKPOINT`, which sounds every 240 pixels – exactly one full lap. Both are clean, zero‑garbage additions that make the race feel alive. Under boost the ticks come fast and frantic; at low speed they idle slowly; and every lap is punctuated by a bright confirmation chime.

### Final Polish

The victory bar at the end of the race passes through `correctLuminance`, so its colour matches the player’s trail exactly. The whole game now runs on the new display architecture without a single reference to the old `DisplayObject`. Race is fully playable, fully visual, and fully audible – and the migration path for Stack, Slots, SimonSays, and all future games is proven and ready.
