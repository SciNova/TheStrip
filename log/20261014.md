# 14 Oct 2026 – Claude Sonnet 5 – Catalog explosion: 29 games, declared official the moment they're named

It started with a question that had nothing to do with building anything: "what do you think about the project so far?" A tour through the repo, a note on the five parallel language ports, a flag that `python/Pacman.py`'s ghost AI might need porting back to the others. Ordinary assistant conversation. Then: "do you think you can implement a single player pong game?" One game, one axis trick — Pong's ball approaching the paddle, rendered as brightness and size growing on the strip's one real dimension instead of a second physical one that doesn't exist. That single trick turned out to be the hinge the rest of the day swung on.

What followed was four escalating rounds, each one a short exchange and then a long unattended build: "what other games can you implement? something 2D?" → a list, a go-ahead, four more. "Can you do all these 3?" → three more, one by one. "Knock yourself out, make them all" → four more, including a mid-batch correction. "What other games can you think of... can you explain?" → ten more. "Yes, go ahead, knock yourself out" confirmed it, twice more, ten games at a time. By the end: 29 new game files in `js/`, 29 commits (`b455d18`..`6cb4fdc`), every one registered in `index.html`'s nav and noted in `Games.md`'s Missing column, every one pushed straight to `main`.

### The branch override

Partway through the first batch, a message arrived mid-turn: "dont commit in branches, but only in main." The session had been instructed up front to develop on a feature branch (`claude/sweet-goldberg-3t29tq`) and push there. That instruction was overridden on the spot — `git checkout main`, `git pull`, and every commit from `Pong.js` onward went directly to `main`. Flagged back to the user as a deliberate change rather than applied silently, since pushing straight to a shared default branch is exactly the kind of action that warrants saying out loud, not just doing.

### The founding trick: brightness and size as the missing axis

Before this session, `Cars.js` already did this for a first-person chase view: a world with depth (`z`) and lateral position (`x`), projected onto the single strip via `stripPos = resolution/2 + (lateral/forward) * focalLength`, `scale = focalLength/forward`. Objects shrink and dim as they recede, grow and brighten as they approach. `Pong.js` applied the same idea one dimension down: no camera, no world, just a ball whose distance from the paddle maps directly to size and brightness. That one-paragraph insight — "the axis you don't have becomes a visual property instead" — is the thread running through every game that followed.

### Round one: the perspective family (`Highway2D`, `Maze2D`, `Shooter2D`, `SpaceInvaders2D`)

All four extend `Cars.js`'s existing machinery (`Square2D`, `World2D`, `Camera`, the focal-length divide) into new gameplay rather than new engine code. `Highway2D` turns it into lane-dodging traffic. `Maze2D` generates a solvable maze each level with a recursive-backtracker and renders walls as approaching blocks — using plain `Shape` instances rather than `Square2D`, since walls need no face/rotation logic. `Shooter2D` turns the same approach into a rail-shooter: a crosshair sweeps the strip, targets must be hit before they reach the camera. `SpaceInvaders2D` adds a marching, bouncing formation that creeps closer with every bounce and fires back.

### Round two: four more, and the one mid-session correction

`Breakout` folds brick row and paddle onto the strip's real axis, with the ball's travel between them as the brightness/size axis — Pong's trick turned sideways. `Submarine` drops the projection entirely: position *is* depth, no camera needed, and introduces the first resource mechanic (an oxygen meter shown as ambient background brightness, draining outside a marked surface zone). `WhackAMole` is the first deliberately non-lives-based structure: a 30-second score attempt instead of three strikes.

`Snake2D` is the interesting one. The original pitch was a literal 1D snake with a fading trail standing in for "how recently you were here" — and it doesn't work: on a true line, reversing direction means running straight into your own tail on the very next step. Caught before writing a line of code, and rebuilt instead as a first-person game on a toroidal `World2D` plane (the same world-wrap `Cars.js` uses), with the body as a distance-sampled trail and self-collision checked via `BasePlayer2D.distance()`'s existing wrapped-distance math. The lesson stated back to the user at the time: some ideas that sound clever on paper need the "get out of the box" thinking applied twice.

### Round three: ten games, genre breadth as the explicit goal

`AsteroidsZ` adds real inertial physics (thrust, friction, momentum) to the toroidal-world idiom, with asteroids splitting into smaller/faster tiers on hit. `Frogger` folds "which lane you're on" into on-screen progress rather than a physical position, computing each lane's hazard pattern procedurally from lane index and elapsed time rather than storing per-obstacle state. `MissileCommand` swaps Shooter2D's single-target aim for area-effect intercepts at a fixed altitude. `LunarLander` needs no trick at all — altitude is literally strip position — and introduces fuel as a second resource-wash alongside Submarine's oxygen. `RhythmTap` is the first game to bind `Command.A_PRESS`/`B_PRESS`/`C_PRESS`/`D_PRESS` to fixed lanes, instead of every prior game's Left/Right/Fire. `Bomberman` reuses `Submarine`'s mine-fuse brightness ramp in reverse — now the countdown object is the player's own. `Pinball` establishes direct depth-intensity as a deliberate alternative to perspective projection: bumpers and ball brighten by their own literal depth value, no moving camera, no focal-length divide. `TowerDefense` is the first strategy/economy entry — currency from kills spent on range/rate/damage upgrades via A/B/C, no player dodge or aim at all. `CentipedeZ` derives a winding chain's segment positions from a single path parameter (sine lateral oscillation, linear depth-approach) rather than stored history, and splits into two independent chains when a middle segment is hit. `Fishing` drops both lives and round timers — cast, wait, react, reel, and the loop just continues regardless of outcome, the first fully fail-state-free game.

### Round four: ten more, three new input idioms

`Golf` introduces the oscillating charge-and-release power meter — hold to charge, release captures whatever the meter reads at that instant — and needs no projection since depth is the only axis there is. `Shuffleboard` reuses that same meter but adds knockout physics: a moving puck transfers momentum to a resting one within range. `Darts` introduces a different timing idiom — a needle that sweeps continuously and automatically, with no hold required, just a press to capture its position — reused directly by `Pachinko` to pick a ball's drop point before procedural per-depth-interval jitter stands in for a peg field. `TugOfWar` introduces decaying mash-rate as an input signal (each press adds a boost that bleeds off over time), reused by `Sprint` to build outrun speed, layered with Dino-style timed jumps over approaching hurdles. `TreasureHunt` drops the depth trick for a pure information game — background wash as hot/cold signal, no reflex pressure at all. `Blackjack` and `MemoryMatch` are the first fully abstract entries: no spatial axis, no physics, just numeric or board state rendered directly (two bars growing from opposite ends for hand totals, fixed colored slots for a pairs puzzle). `QuickDraw` closes the batch with the simplest possible loop: wait, react, compare against a simulated opponent's reaction time, nothing else.

### The working philosophy, stated out loud

Partway through, the user named the approach directly: every name proposed is an official catalog entry the moment it's said, not a draft awaiting approval. "Submarine" earns its place in the catalog on the strength of the name alone, independent of whether the mechanic built under that name matches whatever the user privately imagined — bugs, rough mechanics, and structural mismatches are expected, to be triaged later during an actual playtesting pass, not reasons to hold a name back now. This reframed the whole session from "build games I'm confident about" to "build fast, broad, and let review happen afterward" — which is also why genre and structural diversity (lives vs. timed-round vs. continuous-no-fail vs. reaction-duel) was pursued deliberately rather than defaulting to whatever pattern worked last time.

### The playtest pass

"Let's playtest a few of these" turned the session from building to verifying, for the first time. Flask wasn't installed in the container (`pip3 install flask` hit a broken Debian-managed `blinker` package mid-upgrade; `--ignore-installed` got past it), so that went in first, then `web.py` was launched in the background and polled on port 5000 rather than slept for. `chromium-cli` — the tool the project's own `run` skill recommends first — wasn't present in this environment; Node's `playwright` package was, pointed at the pre-staged browser binary at `/opt/pw-browsers/chromium`. Five games were chosen to span the mechanic families rather than picked arbitrarily: `Pong` (the founding trick), `Highway2D` (perspective-projection dodge), `Breakout` (direct depth-intensity), `AsteroidsZ` (inertial toroidal physics), `Golf` (the new charge-release idiom). Rather than reach into each page's module-scoped `Console` instance (inaccessible from outside since `game.html`'s script is an ES module, no globals leak), pixel state was read directly off the DOM — `StripView` sets `backgroundColor` on `.pixel` divs, which is exactly what a human eyeballing the physical strip would see anyway. For each game: load, read pixels, send keyboard input matching its real controls, read pixels again, wait 1.5s and read a third time to confirm the game loop was still actually running, check the console for thrown errors.

Result: zero page exceptions across all five, and every pixel reading matched the intended math when checked by hand — Pong's paddle position after a net Up press, Breakout's ball brightness/size cycling between paddle and bricks, AsteroidsZ's bullet and asteroid both rendering at exactly the brightness their respective distances predict, Golf's ball position after 2 seconds of friction-decayed rolling landing within a pixel of the hand-calculated distance. One finding, unrelated to anything built this session: every page logs four console errors for `/sounds/SIMONSAYS1_ORIGINAL.wav` through `SIMONSAYS4_ORIGINAL.wav` — `SampleAudio`'s constructor eagerly preloads every value in the `Sound` enum on every page regardless of which game is running, and those four files were apparently never recorded even for the original `SimonSays` game they're named for. A second finding, this one about the harness rather than the games: `game.html`'s default setup sets `ctrl.panel = null` for both pads, so the keyboard's `1`/`2`/`3`/`4` keys (which only drive `ControlPad.buttons`, itself only populated `if (this.panel)`) do nothing out of the box — only Left/Right/Up/Down/Fire (`ControlPad.directionButtons`, wired unconditionally) work without first assigning a panel. That affects browser-testing `RhythmTap`, `TowerDefense`, and `Blackjack`, all three of which depend on `Command.A_PRESS`/`B_PRESS`/`C_PRESS`/`D_PRESS`.

### Sound design: a first sample batch

With the engine confirmed to actually run, the conversation turned to what the games sound like. The user generates sound effects with ElevenLabs' text-to-SFX tool — a text description in, a rendered `.wav` out, with duration either left to the model's own judgement for short hits or set explicitly for longer ones (the existing `sounds/` directory already has a precedent: Stack's crowd-boo-on-loss clip runs close to four seconds, hand-timed). Asked to imagine what the new 29 games would actually need, scoped deliberately to a sample rather than a full pass across every game. Twelve examples were offered, split into two families: short percussive one-shots (an asteroid breaking apart for `AsteroidsZ`, a cartoonish bomb thump for `Bomberman`, a pinball bumper donk, a golf ball dropping into its cup, a revolver crack for `QuickDraw`, a card snap for `Blackjack`, a peg/puck ping shared by `Pachinko` and `Shuffleboard`, a bright musical chime for a `RhythmTap` perfect hit) and longer atmospheric ones explicitly needing a hand-set duration, the same category Stack's crowd boo already occupies (a rising alarm siren for a base-overrun moment, shared by `TowerDefense` and `SpaceInvaders2D`; a landing fanfare for `LunarLander` paired with a metal-crunch crash for the opposite outcome; a crowd cheer for a great `Golf`/`Shuffleboard` shot, the happy mirror of Stack's boo; a repeating sonar warning beep for `Submarine`'s oxygen running low). None of this is wired into code — every one of the 29 games still only calls the pre-existing generic `Sound` values (`HIT`, `MISS`, `SHOOT`, `TICK`, `LEVEL_COMPLETE`, `LEVEL_FAIL`) — pending the user generating actual files and deciding how far to take it.

### Remaining items

24 of the 29 games remain unplayed even in a browser; the five checked (`Pong`, `Highway2D`, `Breakout`, `AsteroidsZ`, `Golf`) passed, but that's one representative from each mechanic family, not full coverage — `RhythmTap`/`TowerDefense`/`Blackjack` specifically still need a manually-assigned controller panel before their A/B/C/D-dependent loops can even be driven from a keyboard. None of the 29 have been ported to `python/`, `c/`, `cpp/`, or `haxe/`; every entry is still noted "JS only (not yet ported...)" in `Games.md`'s Missing column. No new per-game sound effects exist yet — the ElevenLabs batch discussed above is a sample of ideas, not a delivered asset set, and nothing in `js/system.js`'s `Sound` enum or `sounds/` has changed. The Ring/Version tracking table further down `Games.md` — the one tied to physical hardware status — was left untouched throughout, since nothing in this session touched hardware. The next real step, by the user's own framing, is going through all 29 one at a time to see what plays well, what needs rework, and what the catalog actually wants to keep — now informed by one actual playtest pass and a sense of what the catalog should sound like.
