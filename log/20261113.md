# 13 Nov 2026 – Claude Sonnet 5 – Golf rebuilt from scratch: real 2D projectile physics, a scrolling field, and terrain obstacles

The session opened with a look at the existing `Golf.js` — the original one-shot, one-dimensional charge-and-release game from an earlier session — purely to read it, not to touch it yet. What followed was a long, almost entirely conversational design pass, "forget the current implementation" stated outright early on, working out a completely different concept piece by piece before any code was written: a strip-wide scrolling field many screens long, viewed from the side rather than straight down, with the strip's one physical axis doing double duty as both the ball's horizontal position and, through brightness and a projected shadow, its height off the ground.

The class design settled quickly once asked directly: `Hole` (`location`, `radius`), `Ball` (`location`, `speed`), `Player` (`location`, `ball`, `throttle` — later renamed `power` for consistency). The convention carried over from the rest of the codebase — classes take `game` as their first constructor argument and read `this.game.system.clock.delta` directly inside their own methods rather than threading a `dt` parameter through every call — got corrected once, sharply, after `Ball.update(dt)`/`Player._walk(dt)` were written the wrong way the first time: "what the hell is that walk(dt) crap?? delta should not be passed around thats game.delta for." Fixed immediately, matching how `Darts`'s `Needle.update()` already does it.

The physics came out of a real derivation, not a guess. Real golf's own rules were explained in full first (par, strokes, stroke play vs. match play, the "concede a hole once you can't possibly do better" rule), which is what shaped later the per-hole scorecard idea — strokes owned by the player, linked to a hole index, not stored on the `Hole` itself — and match play as the natural two-player format, net to be built later. For the shot itself: real golf's range formula (`R = v²sin(2θ)/g`) combined with "the longest shot should also be the most efficient one" (45°, the angle that maximizes range for a given speed) to derive a clean, dependency-free relationship: the apex height of that longest shot is exactly `R/4`, independent of gravity or speed entirely — so `height = 1.0` for rendering purposes got defined as that quarter-distance, times a small margin. Early on, angle was derived from distance-to-hole (closer holes flatter, farther holes higher, capping at 45° at the field's full length) — a deliberate choice to keep the single charge-release input doing double duty as both power and implicit club selection. That got reversed later in the session once a real bug surfaced: a "full power" shot at different hole distances produced different actual ranges, because speed was already a pure function of power but angle wasn't — fixed by making angle a function of power too (`angle = MAX_ANGLE × power`), so a given power now always sends the ball the same distance regardless of where the hole happens to be. Horizontal drag was added afterward so a shot with no roll-out afterward wouldn't just stop dead on landing — tied to each shot's own tracked apex rather than a fixed global reference, so short lobs and long bombs both decelerate the same *relative* way.

Rendering reused an existing trick rather than inventing one: the ball's shadow, offset from its true position by height and by which side of the strip's center it's on, is the exact same fisheye projection `Dino.js` already uses for its buildings' shadows — confirmed by reading that file directly before writing the new code. The power meter went through several iterations: first a brightness pulse (rejected as unreadable on 1D LEDs), then a growing/shrinking line, then a fixed-length bar with a dark-gray background and light-gray fill — the background was dropped later ("remove the dark gray background"), the direction reversed to pull backward like a pinball plunger rather than point forward, and its length shortened, ending at a point described unprompted as feeling "like a combination of pinball, with snooker/billiard" — the first clear positive signal that the core mechanic had landed.

A real bug came from exactly this kind of layered change: once "power" and "hole-is-always-centered" state tracking were separated into a `PlayerState` enum (`AIMING`/`WALKING`/`ADVANCING`), a stale `this.holed` flag that only got cleared on the next shot's `release()` caused the camera to cascade through every remaining hole in a single frame the moment the first one was sunk — traced and fixed by clearing the flag the instant it's consumed, not when the next shot starts. A second real correction followed close behind: the player was made to teleport straight onto a sunk ball instead of walking to it, which read as wrong once asked outright "where do you put the ball for hitting the next hole" — reverted so the walk always happens for both a miss and a hit, with only the state reached *after* arriving (`AIMING` to try again, or `ADVANCING` to pan toward the next hole) depending on whether it went in. The eventual resting spot for a sunk ball also isn't the hole itself — the ball visually disappears into it, and the player walks on to a tee spot a fixed fraction past the hole, where a "new" ball (same object, relocated) reappears.

The camera went through the same kind of layered correction. Initially tied only to the `WALKING` state, centering on the midpoint of whichever of `{player, ball, hole}` were relevant; a request to also keep the hole in view during `ADVANCING` (panning toward the *next* hole) reused the identical min/max-of-three-points formula, just pointed at a different pair. Once launch angle stopped being tied to distance, a full-power shot could now travel further than one screen regardless of where the hole was — flagged directly ("the ball could get out of view") — fixed by extending the same centering check to run whenever the ball is flying, not just while the player is walking, since flight had been living entirely inside the `AIMING` state and the camera had no idea it needed to track it.

Terrain came in as its own small hierarchy once the field itself was discussed: a base `Obstacle` class (`location`, `size` in pixels, half-open range) with `Lake`, `Sand`, and `Concrete` as one-line subclasses differing only in a static `COLOR` — and, once walking-speed dependence came up, a parallel static `SPEED_FACTOR` (grass and concrete at full speed, sand at half, water at a quarter). Placement itself was folded into the engine's own existing one-time-build convention, found by being told directly to look at `system.js` rather than guess a name — `Game.playLevel()`'s `buildStage()` hook, already used the same way by `MemoryMatch.js` — rather than the ad-hoc `_buildObstacles()` method it started as. The placement algorithm itself ramps difficulty by gap position along the course (the segment right after hole 1 stays completely clean; sand appears first, then lakes, then concrete with a mandatory grass clearance buffer around it, never touching another obstacle directly), sizes growing the further along the course they sit.

A sound-design detour closed the substantive work: ElevenLabs text-to-SFX prompts for footstep foley, one isolated step per surface rather than a looping walk cycle, so pace and rhythm can stay entirely code-controlled — `"single footstep on grass/sand/concrete, close mic, dry foley, no reverb"`, and for the lake specifically, steering away from a literal translation of the Hebrew "שלולית" (puddle) toward "wading through shallow water," since a puddle reads as too thin a splash for a lake crossing. Not yet generated or wired in — scoped as a next step, pending an ElevenLabs API key.
