# 13 Nov 2026 – Claude Sonnet 5 – Golf rebuilt from scratch: real 2D projectile physics, a scrolling field, and terrain obstacles

We opened the session by reading the existing `Golf.js` — the original one-shot, one-dimensional charge-and-release game from an earlier session — purely to look at it, not to touch it yet. What followed was a long, almost entirely conversational design pass: we set the current implementation aside early on and worked out a completely different concept piece by piece before writing any code — a strip-wide scrolling field many screens long, viewed from the side rather than straight down, with the strip's one physical axis doing double duty as both the ball's horizontal position and, through brightness and a projected shadow, its height off the ground.

### Classes and the dt convention

The class design settled quickly once we asked about it directly: `Hole` (`location`, `radius`), `Ball` (`location`, `speed`), `Player` (`location`, `ball`, `throttle` — later renamed `power` for consistency). We'd broken the codebase's own convention at first — classes take `game` as their first constructor argument and read `this.game.system.clock.delta` directly inside their own methods, rather than threading a `dt` parameter through every call — writing `Ball.update(dt)`/`Player._walk(dt)` the wrong way; we caught and fixed that immediately, matching how `Darts`'s `Needle.update()` already does it.

### Projectile physics, derived not guessed

We worked the physics from a real derivation, not a guess, starting by laying out real golf's own rules in full — par, strokes, stroke play vs. match play, the rule that lets a player concede a hole once they can't possibly do better — which later shaped the per-hole scorecard idea: strokes owned by the player, linked to a hole index, not stored on the `Hole` itself, with match play as the natural two-player format, left to build later. For the shot itself, we combined real golf's range formula (`R = v²sin(2θ)/g`) with the fact that the longest shot is also the most efficient one (45°, the angle that maximizes range for a given speed) to derive a clean, dependency-free relationship: the apex height of that longest shot is exactly `R/4`, independent of gravity or speed entirely, so we defined `height = 1.0` for rendering purposes as that quarter-distance, times a small margin. We started by deriving angle from distance-to-hole (closer holes flatter, farther holes higher, capping at 45° at the field's full length), a deliberate choice to keep the single charge-release input doing double duty as both power and implicit club selection — then reversed that once a real bug surfaced: a full-power shot at different hole distances produced different actual ranges, because speed was already a pure function of power but angle wasn't. We fixed it by making angle a function of power too (`angle = MAX_ANGLE × power`), so a given power now always sends the ball the same distance regardless of where the hole happens to be. We added horizontal drag afterward so a shot wouldn't just stop dead on landing with no roll-out, tying it to each shot's own tracked apex rather than a fixed global reference, so short lobs and long bombs both decelerate the same relative way.

### Shadow rendering and the power meter

For rendering, we reused an existing trick rather than inventing one: the ball's shadow, offset from its true position by height and by which side of the strip's center it's on, is the exact same fisheye projection `Dino.js` already uses for its buildings' shadows — we confirmed this by reading that file directly before writing the new code. The power meter went through several iterations: first a brightness pulse, which we rejected as unreadable on 1D LEDs, then a growing/shrinking line, then a fixed-length bar with a dark-gray background and light-gray fill. We dropped the background later, reversed the direction to pull backward like a pinball plunger rather than point forward, and shortened its length — landing on something that unprompted felt like a combination of pinball with snooker or billiards, the first clear sign the core mechanic had worked.

### Camera and state-machine bugs

A real bug came out of exactly this kind of layered change: once we separated the player's power and hole-is-always-centered state tracking into a `PlayerState` enum (`AIMING`/`WALKING`/`ADVANCING`), a stale `this.holed` flag — only cleared on the next shot's `release()` — caused the camera to cascade through every remaining hole in a single frame the moment the first one was sunk. We traced and fixed it by clearing the flag the instant it's consumed, not when the next shot starts. A second real correction followed close behind: we'd made the player teleport straight onto a sunk ball instead of walking to it, which read as wrong once we asked ourselves directly where the ball actually needs to be for hitting the next hole; we reverted that so the walk always happens for both a miss and a hit, with only the state reached after arriving (`AIMING` to try again, or `ADVANCING` to pan toward the next hole) depending on whether it went in. The eventual resting spot for a sunk ball also isn't the hole itself — the ball visually disappears into it, and the player walks on to a tee spot a fixed fraction past the hole, where the same ball object reappears, relocated.

The camera went through the same kind of layered correction. We'd tied it only to the `WALKING` state at first, centering on the midpoint of whichever of `{player, ball, hole}` were relevant; once we also wanted the hole kept in view during `ADVANCING` (panning toward the next hole), we reused the identical min/max-of-three-points formula, just pointed at a different pair. Once launch angle stopped being tied to distance, a full-power shot could travel further than one screen regardless of where the hole was, risking the ball going out of view; we fixed it by extending the same centering check to run whenever the ball is flying, not just while the player is walking, since flight had been living entirely inside the `AIMING` state with nothing keeping the camera aware it needed to track it.

### Terrain obstacles

Terrain came in as its own small hierarchy once we discussed the field itself: a base `Obstacle` class (`location`, `size` in pixels, half-open range) with `Lake`, `Sand`, and `Concrete` as one-line subclasses differing only in a static `COLOR` — and, once walking-speed dependence came up, a parallel static `SPEED_FACTOR` (grass and concrete at full speed, sand at half, water at a quarter). We folded placement itself into the engine's own existing one-time-build convention — `Game.playLevel()`'s `buildStage()` hook, which we found by going straight to `system.js` rather than guessing a name, already used the same way by `MemoryMatch.js` — rather than the ad-hoc `_buildObstacles()` method it started as. The placement algorithm ramps difficulty by gap position along the course: the segment right after hole 1 stays completely clean, sand appears first, then lakes, then concrete with a mandatory grass clearance buffer around it, never touching another obstacle directly, with sizes growing the further along the course they sit.

### Sound design, not yet built

We closed the substantive work with a sound-design detour: ElevenLabs text-to-SFX prompts for footstep foley, one isolated step per surface rather than a looping walk cycle, so pace and rhythm can stay entirely code-controlled — `single footstep on grass/sand/concrete, close mic, dry foley, no reverb`. For the lake specifically, we steered away from a literal translation of the Hebrew שלולית (puddle) toward wading through shallow water, since a puddle reads as too thin a splash for a lake crossing. None of this is generated or wired in yet — it's scoped as a next step, pending an ElevenLabs API key.
